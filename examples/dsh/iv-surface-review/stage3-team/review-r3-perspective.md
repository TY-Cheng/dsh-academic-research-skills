### Summary Assessment

The paper's central reframing — replacing "is the surface arbitrage-free?" with "which guarantee carries the claim?" — is the question a model validator actually needs to ask, and the four-family taxonomy maps cleanly onto the validation lifecycle: conceptual soundness (what carries the guarantee, and what does it assume), ongoing monitoring (dynamic consistency, out-of-sample stability), and outcomes analysis (violation back-testing). That is a real contribution to practice, not a relabelling. The static/dynamic separation, with the explicit admission that most neural evidence is static-only, is accurate and operationally important, and the paper's evidence grading is unusually honest. My reservation is that the paper stops at the vocabulary. The seven-dimension matrix is offered as a minimum reporting standard but supplies no measurands, protocols, units, tolerances, or pass/fail rules; by the paper's own Table 4, three dimensions have no accepted metric at all. Table 2 gives each family a guarantee and a failure mode but not the evidence standard a validator would demand, although §1.2 promises a map from method family to guarantee, evidence strength, and failure mode. The shared-benchmark agenda has no data-access, input-pipeline, or governance plan — the binding constraints on actually building one. The paper is a sound, well-caveated agenda for practice, but not yet a usable instrument; the corrections are within scope and require no new corpus.

### Strengths

1. **S1 — The guarantee-family reframing is directly usable in model documentation and validation planning.** It converts a binary label into a claim about mechanism and assumptions, which is exactly the structure model risk frameworks require (conceptual soundness, limitations, failure modes), and it lets a validation plan be designed from the failure mechanism rather than from the method name. Evidence: `text: §5.1 "papers that use the same phrase are often making different claims"`.
2. **S2 — Table 2 gives each enforcement family a guarantee class and a main failure mode, which is the table a validator needs to start a test plan.** The statement that penalty methods carry "None at inference" is materially useful, because many papers and vendor documents blur a training-time penalty into an inference-time guarantee. Evidence: `table: Table 2`.
3. **S3 — The paper separates static from dynamic no-arbitrage and shows transparently how thin the dynamic evidence is.** Table 3 reports the dynamic-consistency evidence source by source, which matches validation experience: static violation checks are cheap and near-universal, dynamic consistency is rarely tested, and the paper says so with a table rather than a general remark. Evidence: `table: Table 3`.
4. **S4 — The propositions in Table 6 and the "what would change this map" paragraph give a validator falsifiable tests rather than assertions.** A proposition such as "remove the filter; measure violations" is directly executable as a validation experiment. Evidence: `table: Table 6`.
5. **S5 — The practical message is the right ordering for model risk and is phrased so a desk head can act on it.** Evidence: `text: §6 "choose the guarantee before the model"`.

### Weaknesses

1. **W1 — The seven-dimension evaluation matrix is not operationalizable as a reporting standard.** Table 4 names dimensions and "representative metrics", but no validation team can compute a comparable number across studies from it: there is no definition of the object measured (price error versus implied-volatility error, vega weighting, moneyness grid), no unit, no sampling or filtering convention, no tolerance, and no pass/fail rule. Three of the seven dimensions — dynamic consistency, wing behaviour, interpretability — are conceded in the paper's own gap column to have no accepted metric, so a "minimum reporting standard" would instruct a firm to report quantities that do not yet exist as defined measurements. The paper calls the matrix its practical implication (§4.8) and proposes it as a "minimum reporting standard" (§5.3); without a measurement layer the matrix is a topic list, and the comparability problem it diagnoses survives adoption. **Suggestion:** add a "proposed measurand and protocol" column, or a short specification per dimension (object reported, unit, data fields, example tolerance band, pass/fail or descriptive), publish a report-or-declare template so an unmeasured dimension must be declared with a reason and an owner, and work one example applying the matrix to two families on a single dataset.

2. **W2 — The promised map from method family to guarantee, evidence strength, and failure mode is only two-thirds present.** §1.2 defines the practitioner gap as the absence of "a compact map from method family to guarantee, evidence strength, and failure mode", and aim (c) is to assess the strength of the evidence for each family. Table 2 tabulates guarantee and failure mode, but evidence strength is scattered in prose ("moderate, not settled"; preprint counts) and in Appendix D's VI/VII column, which is source-verification level, not strength of evidence for a guarantee. 3. **W3 — The shared-benchmark agenda is not implementable as stated because its two binding constraints are unaddressed: the input pipeline and data access/governance.** §5.5's first agenda item is to build a public benchmark on a common equity-index dataset, but a surface comparison is only as comparable as the pipeline producing the surface: forward and discount curve construction, quote filtering and staleness rules, de-Americanization for American-style sources (as in Yao et al., 2026), and date/time and moneyness-grid conventions. Two studies can share data and still not be comparable if those choices differ — which is the paper's own diagnosis of the literature. Moreover a "public" benchmark on SPX options faces licensing and redistribution constraints; without a data-access model (consortium access, derived surfaces only, or a calibrated synthetic generator) and a neutral owner with maintenance funding, the first agenda item remains aspirational. **Suggestion:** add an input-specification section to the benchmark design (what must be frozen and how it is documented) and a governance paragraph naming the owner, licensing route, versioning, and failure-reporting commitment. ### Detailed Comments

#### Assumption Audit

- **Explicit assumptions**: The paper is unusually explicit about its premises — "arbitrage-free" is ambiguous, static is necessary but not sufficient, the corpus is abstract-level, the taxonomy is interpretive — and these survive cross-disciplinary scrutiny. One stated assumption deserves more pressure: §1.1 separates method family from enforcement family and notes that a method may use more than one mechanism. That is conceptually correct, but operationally the enforcement mechanism is often a property of training or calibration code and is not identifiable from an abstract or a vendor statement. The paper should say whether it classifies the claimed mechanism or the implemented one; if it is the claimed one, the classification is a claim about rhetoric, which makes the evidence-strength gap (W2) more consequential.
- **Implicit assumptions**: (a) That a shared benchmark is the binding constraint on comparability. I would put the measurement protocol logically first: a common dataset with incomparable measurands does not produce comparability. (b) That the guarantee family is the primary axis of model choice for practitioners. In validation the binding constraints are commonly data availability, vendor support, latency, P&L explain and regulatory approval, with arbitrage one acceptance criterion among several. (c) That the practitioner is a sell-side desk or model validator; buy-side users, exchanges and CCPs, internal audit and supervisors are implied but never named. (d) That the surface is produced from a clean input pipeline, when much observed arbitrage originates in the forward curve, quote cleaning and de-Americanization upstream of construction.
- **Paradigmatic assumptions**: The review paradigm treats published claims as the evidence base and therefore imports publication bias toward positive, best-dimension reporting. The paper notes that negative results may be missing — to its credit — but the map is not weighted for this. The comparison paradigm treats construction methods as comparable objects, whereas they are pipeline components with different data and infrastructure requirements. And the "map" paradigm presumes a map without a measurement layer is actionable; in model risk, an unmeasurable map is a checklist.

#### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

- **Parallel research**: Model risk management has already codified the evidence categories this paper needs: conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, and outcomes analysis (SR 11-7, 2011). Prudential backtesting offers a mature precedent for turning violation counts into tolerance bands (traffic-light approach, Basel Committee, 1996). Measurement science distinguishes a measurand from its measurement procedure (ISO/IEC Guide 98-3:2008) — precisely the layer Table 4 lacks.
- **Borrowing opportunities**: (a) SR 11-7's three-pillar structure as an added column in Table 2 ("minimum validation evidence"); (b) traffic-light violation bands for static arbitrage — green/yellow/red violation rates by moneyness and maturity bucket over a stated sampling period, instead of binary counts; (c) "report-or-declare" reporting, where each of the seven dimensions must carry a value or a declared non-measurement with reason and owner — this makes the matrix usable immediately without first resolving the missing-metric problem; (d) reframing "interpretability" as "auditability/verifiability" so the dimension measures whether an independent validator can reproduce the constraint check, not whether a human can explain a neural network.
- **Methodological borrowing**: Champion–challenger benchmarking and repair-rule ablation from model risk practice; stress-period replay (for example, replaying a stressed volatility regime) as the natural operationalization of the paper's "distribution shift" concern; independent reimplementation of the violation check from public code as the strongest available admissibility test.

#### Practical Impact

- **Real-world application**: The guarantee-family vocabulary is directly usable in model documentation and validation plans: each surface model should state its family, the mechanism, the assumption the mechanism makes, the failure mode, and the evidence. A validator can then design tests from the failure mode rather than from the method name, and procurement can ask a vendor which mechanism carries the claim. This is the paper's strongest practical contribution and it is genuine — but it is a documentation aid, not the reporting standard the paper announces.
- **Implementation feasibility**: The barriers are the absence of protocols and tolerances (W1), black-box vendor models whose enforcement mechanism is undisclosed and whose guarantee cannot be independently verified, input-pipeline heterogeneity, and benchmark data licensing (W3). The feasible first step is small: publish a reference violation-check implementation and a one-page report-or-declare template, then let a benchmark grow around them. Attempting the benchmark before the measurement layer repeats the fragmentation the paper criticizes.
- **Stakeholders**: Overlooked parties include buy-side users of surfaces, data and benchmark vendors, exchanges and clearing houses (which use surfaces for margin and settlement), internal audit, supervisors, and users in non-US, less liquid markets. There is also a power asymmetry the paper does not discuss: method owners and vendors benefit from reporting the dimension on which they win, which is an argument for neutral benchmark ownership rather than an academic free-for-all.

#### Broader Implications

- **Ethical dimensions**: Direct ethical stakes are modest, but the "arbitrage-free" label functions as an assurance term in model documentation and vendor materials, and overclaiming it has real consequences for pricing and risk decisions. The AI-use disclosure is exemplary, and the paper is explicit about what an abstract-level review cannot certify.
- **Social impact**: Indirect but real — pricing and risk models at systemically important institutions feed capital, margin and disclosure, so fragmented evaluation raises model risk and the cost of independent validation. That cost falls hardest on smaller firms without in-house validation capacity; a public benchmark and free reporting protocol would reduce the asymmetry. The SPX/US concentration means the guidance is least validated exactly where data are sparser.
- **Future directions**: (1) the measurement layer first — measurands, tolerance bands, reference implementation; (2) a dynamic-consistency test framed as an outcomes-analysis backtest against realized prices rather than as an abstract joint-martingale property; (3) transfer of the guarantee-family frame to other shape-constrained surfaces (credit curves, correlation, energy); (4) auditability metrics that measure the cost of independent verification.

### Cross-Disciplinary Reading Recommendations

- **Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System & Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (2011), SR 11-7 *Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management*** — supplies the conceptual-soundness / ongoing-monitoring / outcomes-analysis structure into which the four guarantee families fit, and states the evidentiary standard the paper's Table 2 lacks.
- **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (1996), *Supervisory framework for the use of "backtesting" in conjunction with the internal models approach to market risk capital requirements*** — a worked precedent for converting violation counts into green/yellow/red tolerance zones, directly transferable to a static-arbitrage violation-rate dimension.
- **International Organization for Standardization (2008), ISO/IEC Guide 98-3 *Uncertainty of measurement — Part 3: Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement*** — borrow the structure, not the statistics: the distinction between a property, its measurand, the measurement procedure, and the tolerance is the missing layer in the seven-dimension matrix.

### Questions for Authors

1. For each of the seven dimensions in Table 4, what is the measurand — the exact object reported, its unit, and the sampling and filtering convention? Could you add one worked example (for instance, butterfly violations defined as a negative risk-neutral density on a stated moneyness grid, with a stated tolerance) so a reader can see what comparability would look like?
2. §1.2 promises a map from method family to guarantee, evidence strength, and failure mode. How should a validation team read evidence strength for each enforcement family from the current tables — is Appendix D's VI/VII column intended as that measure, or is the claim carried only by the prose?
3. If the proposed benchmark frozen only the dataset and not the input pipeline (forward curve, quote filters, de-Americanization, moneyness grid), would the seven dimensions be comparable across studies? What would you require to be frozen?
4. Who should own, fund and maintain the proposed public benchmark, and how would SPX option data licensing and redistribution be handled? Is the expectation an academic consortium, an exchange, or a regulator?
5. Would you consider reporting the evaluation matrix per model *and* per valuation date, since in practice a model's guarantee may hold on average but fail on stressed dates?

### Minor Issues

- Table 4's "interpretability" should be split into "auditability/verifiability" (can a validator independently reproduce the guarantee check?) and "explainability" (can a human explain the model?). These have different validation costs and different owners, and conflating them loses the practical distinction the paper is trying to draw.
- Add one sentence in §5.3 stating what a validator should do when a vendor claims a surface is "arbitrage-free": ask for the enforcement family and commission an independent violation audit. It is the single most actionable sentence the paper's primary reader would use.
- §5.3's model-risk recommendation ("reporting the guarantee family, the static and dynamic test results, and the evaluation dimensions in Table 4") would be more usable as a short reporting template than as prose; as written it is easy for a reader to miss.
- The conclusion's "evaluation is too fragmented to support cross-family ranking" will read to a practitioner as a field-level finding; adding "in the retrieved corpus" keeps the take-away calibrated to what a validation team can rely on.
- Appendix D's "Evidence" column reports source-verification level (VI/VII); calling it evidence *level* and reserving "evidence strength" for what the evidence supports for a guarantee would keep the two distinct and would make W2 easier to fix.
